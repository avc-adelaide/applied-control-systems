\begin{frame}{Compound pendulum}
	We begin by deriving a model of the system.
The nonlinear equation of motion for the pendulum is 
\[\tau(t) = (J_m+J_h+J_p) \ddot{\theta}(t) + D_r \dot{\theta}(t) + m_b r_p g \sin \left(\theta (t)\right), \]
where $\tau$ is the torque applied to the joint and $\theta$ is the angular displacement of the pendulum. The constants in this equation are the moments of inertia of the mass, hub, and pendulum $J_m$, $J_h$, and $J_p$, the mass of the bob $m_b$, centre of gravity of the pendulum $r_p$, viscous damping of the pivot $D_r$, and the gravitational acceleration $g$.\\

Using the small angle approximation, applying the Laplace transform, and rearranging the second-order transfer function for the compound pendulum is 
\[ G(s) = \frac{\theta(s)}{\tau (s)} = \frac{1}{(J_m+J_h+J_p)s^2 + D_r s + m_b r_p g } .\]

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Compound pendulum}
The parameter values for the QUBE compound pendulum are $R_m=8.4\Omega$, $k_m=0.042$ V/(rad/s), $D_r=2\times 10^{-5}$ N.m.s/rad, $J_m = 4\times 10^{-6}$ kg.m$^2$, $J_h = 0.65 \times 10^{-6}$ kg.m$^2$, $m_b=0.03$ kg, $r_b = 0.0127$ m, and $r_p = 0.035$ m.
The moment of inertia of a pendulum is given by the parallel axis theorem as $J_p = m_b r_p^2+ \tfrac{1}{2}m_b r_b^2$.

The second-order transfer function may be transformed into a two-dimensional linear state-space model given by the matrices
\[
\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}
	0 & 1 \\
	-\tfrac{m_br_p g }{J_m+J_h+J_p} & -\left(\tfrac{R_m D_r + k_m^2}{R_m(J_m+J_h+J_p)}\right)
\end{bmatrix},
\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix}
	0 \\ \tfrac{k_m}{R_m(J_m+J_h+J_p)}
\end{bmatrix},
\]  \\
\[
\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix}
	1 & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix}
	0
\end{bmatrix}
\]  
where 
\[\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}
	\theta_m \\ \dot{\theta}_m
\end{bmatrix}
\quad , \quad \mathbf{y} = \theta_m \quad  \text{and} \quad u=v_m.\]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Time domain analysis}

The average logarithmic decrement is calculated across \(N\) peaks in the
time-series window (with \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) being the first ratio,
\(x_2\) and \(x_3\) the second, and so on):
%
\begin{equation}
    \delta
    =
    \frac{1}{N-1}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}
    \ln\left(\frac{x_i}{x_{i+1}}\right).
\end{equation}

The ratio of successive amplitudes gives the damping ratio of an
underdamped system:
%
\begin{equation}
    \zeta
    =
    \frac{\delta}
    {\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}}.
\end{equation}

This damping ratio is used to convert the observed damped natural frequency (underdamped system)
into the undamped natural frequency:
%
\begin{equation}
    \omega_n
    =
    \frac{\omega_d}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}.
\end{equation}

\end{frame}

\SUBCONCEPT{Controlling the plant}

\begin{frame}{Pole placement}

Given the transfer function model of the compound pendulum, one method of control is to design state feedback such that the resulting poles of the closed loop system provide a desired response

For a general second-order system, the pair of complex poles are given by  
\[s=\omega_n (-\zeta_n \pm j \sqrt{1-\zeta_n^2})\]

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=5cm]{prac5_poles}
	\caption{Plot of a pair of complex poles.}
\end{figure}


\end{frame}



\begin{frame}{Pole placement}
Using this controller we are changing the dynamic response of the system (the damping ratio is proportional to the damping).
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Calculate the position of the desired poles given that we require a damping ratio of $\zeta_n=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}$ with a natural frequency of $\omega_n=10$ rad/s
	\item Calculate the $A$ and $B$ matrices in MATLAB and use the command \texttt{K=place(A,B,p)} to determine the required gains 
	\item Implement this controller in Simulink by multiplying the appropriate states with the corresponding gain values (you will have to estimate the angular velocity $\dot{\theta}$ using a low-pass filter and a derivative block)
	\item Repeat the impulse response from part one, comparing the closed-loop plant response with the state-space model (requiring $A_{CL} = A - BK$). \textbf{IMPORTANT:} The state-space model does not require feedback from the plant.
	\item Calculate the theoretical damped natural frequency $\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$
\end{enumerate}

\SUBCONCEPT{Frequency response}

\begin{frame}{Response to a sinusoid input}
With a controller implemented we would like to understand how well our controller can follow a given input. One way to assess this, that gives us more information than just looking at the system's response to a step input, is to input a periodic signal and analyse the phase and magnitude of the output.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Replace the Step block (configured as an impulse response) in Simulink with a Sine Wave block, set the frequency somewhere between $1$ and $10$ rad/s with an amplitude of $1$.
	\item Run the Simulink model and assess how well the controller follows this signal. This requires plotting both the input (setpoint) and output (current angle) signals on one Scope. You do not need to plot the state-space response.
    \item Repeat for a few frequencies to observe the setpoint tracking accuracy (amplitude and phase)
    \item Compare the response to the uncontrolled (open-loop) system
\end{enumerate}
